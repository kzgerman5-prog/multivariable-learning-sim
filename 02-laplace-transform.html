CHAPTER 02 · FINDING THE EXPONENTIALS INSIDE

The Laplace transform

A signal in time becomes a function on the s-plane. Its poles point to the exponential motions hiding inside.

02 / 04

1. A machine that tests for exponentials

Suppose a signal is a sum of exponential ingredients, such as f(t)=3e−t−2e2t. How might we reveal those ingredients? Pick a test exponent s, multiply the signal by e−st, and add the result over all future time. This is the one-sided Laplace transform:

THE LAPLACE TRANSFORMF(s)=ℒ{f}(s)=∫0∞ f(t)e−st dtHere s=σ+iω is a complex test value. The integral may converge only for some s.

For an ingredient f(t)=eat, choose s near a. The integrand becomes e(a−s)t. At s=a, the exponents cancel, leaving the constant 1, whose integral over an infinite interval diverges. Nearby, the transform becomes very large. This blow-up is a pole at s=a: a marker for the exponential ingredient.

The sign in e−st is intentional. The test cancels the signal when s matches its exponent. It also makes decaying exponentials easy to handle: for a real a, the integral converges when Re(s)>a.

2. The transform of an exponential is a pole

For Re(s−a)>0, evaluate the integral directly:

ℒ{eat} = ∫0∞ e−(s−a)tdt = [−e−(s−a)t/(s−a)]0∞ = 1/(s−a).

This expression has a simple pole at s=a. If f(t)=Σ ckeaₖt, linearity gives F(s)=Σ ck/(s−aₖ), so every exponential contributes a pole. A pole records a location and a strength (its residue); in simple cases, that strength is exactly the exponential coefficient.

For example, cos(ωt)=(eiωt+e−iωt)/2. Therefore ℒ{cos(ωt)}=s/(s²+ω²), with poles at s=±iω. They sit on the imaginary axis because a pure sinusoid neither grows nor decays.

3. Complex vector sums and convergence

When s=σ+iω, the test exponential is e−σte−iωt. The factor e−iωt rotates each tiny contribution, while e−σt changes its length. So the integral can be pictured as a continuous sum of rotating arrows. Changing ω changes how quickly they turn; changing σ changes how strongly late-time arrows are weighted.

The real part matters because it controls convergence. If f(t)=eat, then |e−stf(t)|=e(a−σ)t. The area is finite precisely when σ>a. That half-plane is called the region of convergence (ROC). A formula such as 1/(s−a) can be written beyond the ROC, but the original integral still converges only to the right of the pole.

Analytic continuation means extending the same complex function using its algebraic/analytic structure beyond where the defining integral converges. It can reveal poles that the integral region cannot reach. Keep the distinction clear: the continued expression is useful information about the signal, but it is not a newly convergent integral.

4. Reading poles responsibly

For a rational transform F(s), poles often reveal exponential pieces, but with an important qualification: the pole locations alone do not always determine the signal. Coefficients matter, repeated poles introduce powers of t, and the ROC distinguishes signals that can share the same algebraic expression. For instance, the bilateral transform treats signals over all real time and uses a vertical strip of convergence; the one-sided transform used here integrates from zero to infinity and is the natural tool for initial-value problems.

Inverse Laplace transforms use partial fractions to rewrite a complicated F(s) as simple pieces with known inverse pairs. A term A/(s−a) becomes Aeat; a repeated pole A/(s−a)² becomes At eat. The transform is valuable because structure in the s-plane turns into understandable behavior in time.

CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Three problems, fully worked

01 Find ℒ{4e−2t−3et} and its region of convergence.

Step 1 — transform each exponential. ℒ{eat}=1/(s−a), so the first term has a=−2 and the second has a=1.

F(s)=4/(s+2)−3/(s−1).

Step 2 — require both integrals to converge. The first requires Re(s)>−2; the second requires Re(s)>1. Both must hold, so the intersection is Re(s)>1. The poles are −2 and 1, but the ROC lies to the right of the rightmost pole.

02 Find ℒ{cos(3t)} from Euler’s formula and identify the poles.

Step 1 — express cosine as two exponentials. cos(3t)=(e3it+e−3it)/2.

Step 2 — transform both parts.

F(s)=1/2·1/(s−3i)+1/2·1/(s+3i)=s/(s²+9).

The poles are s=±3i. The ROC is Re(s)>0 for the one-sided transform because the sinusoid does not decay, so the test factor must supply decay. The poles describe persistent oscillation at 3 radians per time unit.

03 Invert F(s)=2/(s+1)−1/(s−2), then find where its defining integral converges.

Step 1 — match inverse pairs. The pole at −1 contributes 2e−t; the pole at 2 contributes −e2t. Thus f(t)=2e−t−e2t.

Step 2 — intersect the convergence conditions. The first piece requires Re(s)>−1. The growing second piece requires Re(s)>2. Therefore the complete signal’s one-sided transform converges only when Re(s)>2. The rational formula can be analytically continued elsewhere except at its poles, but that does not make the original integral converge there.
