The Problem with Smooth Waves

While basic trigonometric functions like sines and cosines are continuous, smooth, and predictable, physical systems often feature sharp transitions, sudden shocks, or discontinuous boundaries—such as a square wave or a step function.

A single sine wave is far too smooth to approximate a sharp corner or a vertical drop. To represent discontinuous functions, we need a mechanism that can combine multiple frequencies to build steep vertical walls while maintaining flat horizontal plateaus.

Test Your Knowledge

1. Evaluating Fourier Coefficients for a Square Wave ▼

Question: Given a square wave $f(x)$ defined as $f(x) = 4 / \pi \sum_{n=1, 3, 5...}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n} \sin(nx)$, find the value of the third harmonic component ($n=3$) when $x = \frac{\pi}{6}$.

Solution:

1. Isolate the $n=3$ term of the series:
$T_3(x) = \frac{4}{\pi} \cdot \frac{1}{3} \sin(3x)$

2. Substitute $x = \frac{\pi}{6}$:
$3x = 3 \left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2}$

3. Evaluate $\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}) = 1$:
$T_3\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{4}{3\pi} \cdot 1 = \frac{4}{3\pi}$

Fourier's Breakthrough

Joseph Fourier made the audacious claim that arbitrary functions could be decomposed into an infinite sum of harmonics. This proposition was initially met with intense skepticism by the leading mathematicians of his day.

The breakthrough lies in the orthogonality of sine and cosine functions. By integrating a target function multiplied against a specific test frequency over a period, all other frequencies cancel out to zero, leaving behind only the coefficient corresponding to that specific harmonic.

1. Orthogonality Property ▼

Question: Evaluate the integral $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin(mx) \sin(nx) \, dx$ for integers $m$ and $n$ where $m \neq n$.

Solution:

1. Use the trigonometric product-to-sum identity:
$\sin(mx)\sin(nx) = \frac{1}{2}\cos((m-n)x) - \frac{1}{2}\cos((m+n)x)$

2. Integrate over $[-\pi, \pi]$:
Since $m \neq n$, both $(m-n)$ and $(m+n)$ are non-zero integers.

3. The integral of cosine over a full period $[-\pi, \pi]$ for non-zero integer frequencies is strictly 0.
Result: 0.

From 1D Waves to 2D Drawings

While Fourier originally analyzed real-valued functions along a 1D spatial axis, the geometric intuition becomes exceptionally clear when extended into the complex plane as a 2D path.

Instead of a wave bouncing up and down along a line, imagine a continuous curve traced in a 2D plane over time. A 1D real signal is merely a special case where motion is constrained strictly to the imaginary or real axis.

The Complex Exponential ($e^{it}$)

At the core of modern Fourier analysis is Euler's formula and the complex exponential function $e^{i\omega t}$.

$e^{it} = \cos(t) + i\sin(t)$

As time $t$ marches forward, $e^{it}$ traces a uniform circular path around the origin of the complex plane at a constant angular velocity. Multiplying this by a constant scales its radius and rotates its starting phase.

Test Your Knowledge

1. Derivative of Complex Exponential ▼

Question: Compute the time derivative $\frac{d}{dt} \left( C e^{i n t} \right)$ where $C$ is a complex constant and $n$ is an integer.

Solution:

1. Apply the chain rule for exponential differentiation:
$\frac{d}{dt} \left( e^{i n t} \right) = i n e^{i n t}$

2. Multiply by the constant $C$:
$\frac{d}{dt} \left( C e^{i n t} \right) = i n C e^{i n t}$

Geometrically, multiplying by $i$ rotates the velocity vector by $90^\circ$ ahead of the position vector.

Controlling the Swarm

To draw intricate curves, a single rotating vector is insufficient. We link an infinite swarm of vectors end-to-end (epicycles), where each vector spins at a different integer frequency $n$.

The position at any time $t$ is given by the sum: $f(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} C_n e^{i n t}$ By adjusting the complex coefficients $C_n$, we control the radius and initial phase angle of each epicycle in the swarm.

Finding the Constants ($C_n$)

How do we find the exact complex coefficient $C_n$ for any given target drawing? The secret lies in a brilliant integration trick.

To isolate a specific frequency $n$, we multiply the entire target function $f(t)$ by $e^{-i n t}$ and integrate over a full period $T$:

$C_n = \frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^{T} f(t) e^{-i n t} \, dt$

This multiplication cancels out the rotation of the target vector for frequency $n$ (making it stand completely still), while all other frequencies spin wildly. When averaged over time, the spinning vectors integrate to zero, leaving behind only the isolated constant $C_n$!

Test Your Knowledge

1. Calculating $C_0$ (Center of Mass) ▼

Question: What physical or geometric interpretation does the coefficient $C_0$ represent for a closed loop path $f(t)$ over $t \in [0, 2\pi]$?

Solution:

1. Set $n = 0$ in the coefficient formula:
$C_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} f(t) e^{0} \, dt = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} f(t) \, dt$

2. This is precisely the integral average of all points along the path $f(t)$.
Interpretation: $C_0$ represents the center of mass (or average offset position) of the entire drawing in the complex plane.

The Final Result

When you combine all these mathematical tools together—calculating the complex coefficients $C_n$ via integration and chaining the rotating vectors tip-to-tail—you obtain a fully functional drawing machine.

Any arbitrary shape, signature, or contour can be recreated with arbitrary precision simply by adding more epicycles to the swarm.

Test Your Knowledge

1. Convergence and Precision ▼

Question: If a target shape contains sharp corners (discontinuities in its tangent vector), how does increasing the number of epicycles $N$ affect the visual fidelity and convergence?

Solution:

1. Smooth portions of a curve converge extremely rapidly with small $N$.

2. At sharp corners, higher frequency epicycles are required to capture high-frequency spatial details.

3. However, due to the Gibbs phenomenon, adding more terms narrows the overshoot ripple near corners but does not completely eliminate the peak overshoot amplitude (~9%), though it confines the error to an infinitesimally narrow region around the corner.
