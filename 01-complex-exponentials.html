CHAPTER 01 · MOTION IN THE COMPLEX PLANE

Complex exponentials

One expression, est, can describe growth, rotation, and spiraling. The geometry is the calculus.

01 / 04

1. The exponential that chases itself

On the real number line, the function x(t)=et has a remarkable property: its rate of change equals its current value. In symbols, x′(t)=x(t). Its position tells you its velocity. At x=1 it moves at 1 unit per second; at x=10 it moves at 10 units per second. That is why the graph gets steeper as it rises: the motion speeds itself up.

There is nothing special about starting at 1. If x′=x and x(0)=x₀, then x(t)=x₀et. Multiplying by a constant changes the starting amount, not the rule. Replace the growth rate by a real number a and x′=ax has the solution x=x₀eat: positive a grows, negative a decays, and a=0 stays put.

THE DEFINING MOTIONx′(t)=a x(t)   ⟹   x(t)=x(0)eatExponential functions are the natural motions of systems whose velocity is proportional to their position.

2. What does an imaginary exponent do?

Now let the position be a point in the plane, represented by a complex number z=x+iy. Multiplication by i turns every vector a quarter-turn counterclockwise: i(x+iy)=−y+ix. So if the motion obeys z′=iz, its velocity always points 90° ahead of its position. The velocity is perpendicular to the radius, so it cannot make the point move closer to or farther from the origin. It must move around a circle.

The solution is z(t)=eitz(0). Starting at 1 gives eit=cos t+i sin t. This is Euler’s formula, and it is a geometric description: the real and imaginary parts are the horizontal and vertical coordinates of a point rotating around the unit circle. The angular speed is one radian per unit time. At t=π the point has traveled halfway around, so eiπ=−1, or eiπ+1=0.

More generally, eiωt rotates at angular frequency ω. A complex amplitude A=Reiφ sets the radius R and starting angle φ. Taking the real part gives the ordinary oscillation R cos(ωt+φ). The complex number is a convenient rotating arrow; its real projection is the familiar back-and-forth wave.

EULER’S FORMULAeiθ=cos θ+i sin θMultiplication by eiθ rotates a vector by θ without changing its length.

3. The s-plane combines decay and rotation

Let the exponent s itself be complex: s=σ+iω. Then est=eσteiωt. The real part σ controls radius: positive σ spirals outward, negative σ spirals inward, and zero σ stays on a circle. The imaginary part ω controls the turning speed. Complex exponentials are therefore spirals whose radial and angular motions can be tuned independently.

Plot s=σ+iω as the point (σ,ω) on the s-plane. Every point specifies a distinct motion est. A point far left means rapid decay; a point above or below the real axis means oscillation; the distance from the horizontal axis sets its angular frequency. This picture becomes a language for describing the behavior of physical systems.

σ < 0decays
σ = 0keeps oscillating
σ > 0grows

4. A spring finds its own exponentials

For a mass attached to a spring with friction, Newton’s law gives m x″+c x′+k x=0. Here m is mass, c is damping, and k is spring stiffness. Exponentials are useful because differentiating est only multiplies it by s: its derivatives are s est, s²est, and so on. Guess x=est and substitute:

(m s²+c s+k)est=0   ⟹   m s²+c s+k=0.

The quadratic is called the characteristic equation. If its roots are s=−α±iω, then real solutions are combinations of e−αtcos(ωt) and e−αtsin(ωt): oscillations wrapped in a shrinking envelope. The negative real part describes energy lost to friction; the imaginary part describes the oscillation. This is not a trick that ignores physics: it is the exact motion expressed in the natural building blocks of differentiation.

In general, when the roots are distinct, x(t)=C₁es₁t+C₂es₂t. For real coefficients and a complex-conjugate pair, choose constants so the final motion is real. Later, the Laplace transform will make this exponential decomposition systematic, even when the equation has an external force.

CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Three problems, fully worked

01 Solve z′=(−2+3i)z, z(0)=2. Describe the motion and find z(π/3).

Step 1 — use the exponential solution. For z′=sz, the solution is z(t)=z(0)est. Here s=−2+3i, so

z(t)=2e(−2+3i)t=2e−2t(cos 3t+i sin 3t).

Step 2 — read the geometry. The radius is 2e−2t, so it decays with time constant 1/2. The angle is 3t, so the point rotates counterclockwise at 3 radians per unit time.

Step 3 — evaluate at t=π/3. The angle is π and the radius is 2e−2π/3. Therefore z(π/3)=−2e−2π/3+0i. Numerically this is about −0.247+0i.

02 Solve x″+4x′+13x=0 with x(0)=1, x′(0)=0. Identify the decay rate and frequency.

Step 1 — characteristic equation. Set x=est. Then s²+4s+13=0, with roots s=−2±3i.

Step 2 — form a real solution. A conjugate pair −α±iω gives x=e−αt(A cos ωt+B sin ωt). Thus x=e−2t(A cos 3t+B sin 3t).

Step 3 — impose the initial position. x(0)=A=1. Differentiating gives x′(0)=−2A+3B=0, hence B=2/3. So

x(t)=e−2t(cos 3t + (2/3) sin 3t).

The amplitude envelope decays at rate 2, while the angular frequency is 3 rad/unit time. The oscillation frequency in cycles per time unit is 3/(2π).

03 A system satisfies x′=−3x, y′=−3y. Explain why r(t)=√(x²+y²) decays as e−3t, and contrast this with z′=iz.

Step 1 — solve each coordinate. x(t)=x₀e−3t and y(t)=y₀e−3t. Therefore

r(t)=√(x₀²e−6t+y₀²e−6t)=e−3t√(x₀²+y₀²).

Step 2 — compare the vector fields. Multiplication by −3 points velocity directly toward the origin, so the radius shrinks. Multiplication by i turns the position by 90°, creating tangential velocity. For z′=iz, the radius stays constant and only the angle changes. In general, z′=(σ+iω)z combines both effects.
