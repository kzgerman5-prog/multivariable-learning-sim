CHAPTER 04 · EXPONENTIALS OF TRANSFORMATIONS

Matrix exponentials

A matrix sets the velocity at every state. Its exponential tells you where that state flows over time.

04 / 04

1. Exponentiate an operation, not a number

For a real number x, the exponential can be defined by its Taylor series: ex=1+x+x²/2!+x³/3!+⋯. To define the exponential of a square matrix A, use the same series, replacing the number 1 by the identity matrix I and each power xⁿ by Aⁿ:

MATRIX EXPONENTIALeAt=I+At+(At)²/2!+(At)³/3!+⋯The identity matrix plays the role of the number 1. The series converges for every finite matrix A and time t.

You cannot multiply the scalar e by a matrix to get this answer. The exponential is a new matrix built from powers of A. As with scalar exponentials, differentiating the series term by term gives d/dt eAt=A eAt=eAtA, and eA·0=I.

2. The solution to a coupled system

Consider a state vector x(t) whose components might be positions, populations, or concentrations. If its velocity is given by a fixed linear rule, x′=Ax, then the solution with starting state x₀ is

x(t)=eAtx₀.

Differentiate: x′=A eAtx₀=A x, exactly the system we wanted, and at t=0 the state is Ix₀=x₀. In a single equation the matrix exponential solves every possible starting state. Its columns are the evolved positions of the coordinate basis vectors.

The matrix A acts locally: at each point x, the arrow Ax is the instantaneous velocity. The matrix exponential acts globally: it advances the whole state by a chosen amount of time. Thinking of eAt as a flow map connects linear algebra to differential equations.

3. Rotation, stretching, and the eigenvector shortcut

A pure rotation by 90 degrees per time unit has generator J=[[0,−1],[1,0]]. Since J²=−I, its powers repeat, and the series groups into the familiar sine and cosine series:

eJt=I cos t+J sin t = [[cos t,−sin t],[sin t,cos t]].

Thus a constant matrix that represents infinitesimal rotation becomes continuous circular motion when exponentiated. Multiplying J by an angular speed ω makes the angle ωt. More generally, adding −αI creates a shrinking spiral: e(−αI+ωJ)t=e−αteωJt, because these two matrices commute.

If A has an eigenvector v with eigenvalue λ, then Av=λv, A²v=λ²v, and so on. Applying the series gives eAtv=eλtv. Each eigenvector direction evolves with its own scalar exponential. When a matrix has enough independent eigenvectors, this observation makes it possible to compute its exponential by diagonalizing it. Complex eigenvalues are fine: conjugate pairs combine into real spirals and oscillations in real systems.

4. More than a diagonal matrix

Not every matrix has a full set of independent eigenvectors, and not every problem is easiest by diagonalization. The Taylor-series definition always makes sense. Another useful fact is e(A+B)t=eAteBt when A and B commute (AB=BA). Without commutation this generally fails—the order of transformations matters.

Matrix exponentials appear throughout physics. Schrödinger’s equation is linear evolution with an imaginary factor; two-level quantum states rotate in a complex state space. In mechanics, a system of coupled first-order equations can be assembled into one vector equation x′=Ax. The eigenvalues tell us whether its natural modes decay, persist, or grow, while eAt gives the actual trajectory for any initial state.

The matrix exponential does not mean that every vector moves along a straight line or rotates as one rigid object. The shape of the flow depends on A: it may shear, stretch, contract, rotate, or combine all of these. The series captures this continuous accumulation of the local action A.

CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Three problems, fully worked

01 Compute eAt for A=[[0,−2],[2,0]], then find x(π/4) from x(0)=(1,0).

Step 1 — recognize the rotation generator. A=2J with J=[[0,−1],[1,0]]. Since J²=−I, the even powers contribute I cos(2t) and odd powers contribute J sin(2t).

eAt=[[cos 2t,−sin 2t],[sin 2t,cos 2t]].

Step 2 — apply the initial state. At t=π/4 the angle is π/2, so eAπ/4 rotates (1,0) to (0,1). The solution is x(t)=(cos 2t,sin 2t).

02 Solve x′=[[2,0],[0,−1]]x with x(0)=(3,4). What happens as t→∞?

Step 1 — exponentiate the diagonal matrix. Its powers stay diagonal, so exponentiate the diagonal entries:

eAt=[[e2t,0],[0,e−t]].

Step 2 — apply the initial state. x(t)=(3e2t,4e−t). The first coordinate grows without bound; the second decays to zero. The origin is unstable because one eigenvalue is positive, even though the other mode is stable.

03 Let A=[[−1,−1],[1,−1]]. Find eAt and describe the trajectory from x(0)=(1,0).

Step 1 — split decay and rotation. Write A=−I+J, where J=[[0,−1],[1,0]]. These commute, so eAt=e−tIeJt.

eAt=e−t[[cos t,−sin t],[sin t,cos t]].

Step 2 — apply the initial state. x(t)=e−t(cos t,sin t). The state rotates counterclockwise at one radian per time unit while its radius decays as e−t. It spirals inward to the origin. The eigenvalues −1±i encode exactly that combination of decay and rotation.
