The Heat Equation Setup

Imagine a solid, one-dimensional rod with an uneven temperature distribution. Some spots are hot, and some are cold. Our goal is to determine how that heat will flow from the warmer spots to the cooler ones, evolving over time.

Instead of trying to immediately find a master formula that gives the exact temperature at any place and time, we start by describing the changing state moment-to-moment. We use derivatives to describe how the system evolves locally, trusting that this will lead us to the global solution.

Test Your Knowledge

1. Boundary Conditions and Steady State ▼

Question: Consider a 1D rod of length $L = 10$. The left end is fixed at $0^\circ\text{C}$ and the right end is fixed at $100^\circ\text{C}$. Over a long period of time ($t \to \infty$), the temperature reaches a "steady state" where it no longer changes with time. What is the temperature function $u(x)$ at this steady state?

Solution:

1. At steady state, the temperature does not change over time. Therefore, the time derivative is zero: $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = 0$.

2. The heat equation is $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$. Setting the left side to zero gives: $0 = \alpha \frac{d^2 u}{dx^2}$ (we use ordinary derivatives since $u$ only depends on $x$ now).

3. Integrating twice with respect to $x$:
$\frac{du}{dx} = C_1$
$u(x) = C_1 x + C_2$

4. This means the steady-state temperature profile is a straight line. Now we apply boundary conditions:

Left bound: $u(0) = 0 \implies C_1(0) + C_2 = 0 \implies C_2 = 0$.

Right bound: $u(10) = 100 \implies C_1(10) + 0 = 100 \implies C_1 = 10$.

Final Answer: $u(x) = 10x$.

Fourier's Contribution

The Heat Equation is the exact problem Joseph Fourier was trying to solve when he made a mathematically shocking discovery. He realized that the math used to describe heat flow naturally gives rise to sine and cosine waves.

Fourier claimed that any arbitrary initial temperature distribution could be constructed simply by adding together a series of sine and cosine waves of different frequencies and amplitudes. Because we know exactly how simple sine waves diffuse over time (they just scale down exponentially), breaking a complex shape into sine waves makes solving the Heat Equation trivial!

Test Your Knowledge

1. The Eigenfunction Property ▼

Question: Why are sine and cosine waves so magically perfectly suited for the Heat Equation? Show what happens when you take the second spatial derivative of $u(x) = \sin(kx)$.

Solution:

Sine and cosine are "eigenfunctions" of the second derivative operator. This means taking their second derivative yields the exact same function back, just scaled by a constant.

1. Let $u(x) = \sin(kx)$.

2. First derivative: $\frac{du}{dx} = k \cos(kx)$.

3. Second derivative: $\frac{d^2u}{dx^2} = -k^2 \sin(kx)$.

Notice that $\frac{d^2u}{dx^2} = -k^2 u(x)$. Because the Heat Equation equates the time derivative to the second spatial derivative, a spatial sine wave means the temperature at every point is changing at a rate exactly proportional to its current value (which creates simple exponential decay in time!).

Partial Derivatives

Because the temperature of the rod depends on two independent variables—where you are on the rod ($x$) and when you check the temperature ($t$)—standard calculus isn't enough. We must use partial derivatives.

  • $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$: How temperature changes as you move along the rod. This is the spatial slope.
  • $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}$: How the temperature of a single, fixed point changes as time progresses. This is the temporal slope.

Test Your Knowledge

1. Calculating Partial Derivatives ▼

Question: Given the 2D function $u(x,t) = e^{-2t} \cos(3x)$, compute the partial derivatives $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}$ and $\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$.

Solution:

When taking a partial derivative with respect to one variable, you treat all other variables as constants.

1. For $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}$: Treat $x$ (and therefore $\cos(3x)$) as a constant.
$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}(e^{-2t}) \cdot \cos(3x) = -2e^{-2t} \cos(3x)$

2. For $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$: Treat $t$ (and therefore $e^{-2t}$) as a constant.
$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = e^{-2t} \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\cos(3x)) = e^{-2t} \cdot (-3\sin(3x)) = -3e^{-2t} \sin(3x)$

3. For $\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$: Differentiate the result above with respect to $x$ again.
$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = -3e^{-2t} \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\sin(3x)) = -3e^{-2t} \cdot (3\cos(3x)) = -9e^{-2t} \cos(3x)$

Reading the Equation

Let's look at the Heat Equation itself. It elegantly connects the two partial derivatives we just discussed.

$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$

The equation states that the rate at which temperature changes over time ($\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}$) is proportional to the second spatial derivative ($\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$).

Intuitively, the second derivative measures concavity. Points where the temperature graph curves sharply downwards (concave down, negative second derivative) are hotter than their surroundings and will rapidly cool down. Points that are concave up will heat up.

Test Your Knowledge

1. Verifying a Solution ▼

Question: Is the function $u(x,t) = e^{-4t} \sin(2x)$ a valid solution to the heat equation $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$? If so, what is the thermal diffusivity constant $\alpha$?

Solution:

We need to plug the function into both sides of the PDE and see if they balance.

1. Calculate LHS: $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -4 e^{-4t} \sin(2x)$

2. Calculate RHS spatial derivatives:
$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 2 e^{-4t} \cos(2x)$
$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = -4 e^{-4t} \sin(2x)$

3. Plug into the PDE:
$-4 e^{-4t} \sin(2x) = \alpha \cdot (-4 e^{-4t} \sin(2x))$

For this equality to hold for all $x$ and $t$, we must have $\alpha = 1$.
Conclusion: Yes, it is a solution, and the constant $\alpha = 1$.

The Discrete Intuition

To truly understand *why* the second derivative determines temperature change, imagine the rod is not a smooth continuum, but a series of discrete, pixelated blocks.

A specific block will heat up if its immediate left and right neighbors are, on average, hotter than it is. It will cool down if its neighbors are colder. The change in temperature is proportional to the difference between the block's current temperature and the average of its neighbors.

1. Calculating a Discrete Step ▼

Question: We have three blocks in a row with temperatures $T_1 = 20^\circ$, $T_2 = 50^\circ$, and $T_3 = 60^\circ$. According to the discrete rule, the new temperature of the middle block is given by $T_2^{new} = T_2 + r(T_1 - 2T_2 + T_3)$, where $r = 0.2$ is our rate constant. What is the new temperature of $T_2$?

Solution:

1. Calculate the "second difference" (which is proportional to the difference between the average of neighbors and the point itself):
Diff $= T_1 - 2T_2 + T_3$
Diff $= 20 - 2(50) + 60 = 20 - 100 + 60 = -20$.

2. This negative value makes sense: the middle block is $50^\circ$, while the average of its neighbors is $(20+60)/2 = 40^\circ$. So $T_2$ should cool down.

3. Apply the rate constant to find the change:
$\Delta T_2 = 0.2 \cdot (-20) = -4$.

4. Find the new temperature:
$T_2^{new} = 50 - 4 = 46^\circ$.

The Second Difference

How does the discrete averaging of neighbors turn into the continuous second derivative? Through the concept of the Second Difference.

If you take a point $x$, and its neighbors $x - \Delta x$ and $x + \Delta x$, you can calculate how much $f(x)$ deviates from the average of its neighbors:

$\text{Deviation} = \frac{f(x-\Delta x) + f(x+\Delta x)}{2} - f(x)$

By doing some algebraic manipulation and letting the distance between neighbors ($\Delta x$) shrink to zero, this exact expression becomes the definition of the second derivative!

PDEs vs. ODEs

What makes a Partial Differential Equation (PDE) fundamentally different from an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE)? It comes down to dimensions and "degrees of freedom."

ODE (Ordinary)

Tracks a finite number of variables over a single dimension (usually time). Example: Tracking the $(x,y,z)$ coordinates of 3 planets over time requires 9 functions.

PDE (Partial)

Models an infinite continuum of values across multiple dimensions (space and time). Example: The Heat equation tracks the temperature of infinite microscopic points along a rod.

Higher Dimensions (The Laplacian)

The beauty of the Heat Equation is that expanding it from a 1D rod to a 2D plate or a 3D block is incredibly straightforward.

You simply add the second spatial derivatives for the other dimensions. This sum is so common in physics it gets its own special symbol and name: The Laplacian ($\nabla^2$).

$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha \left( \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial z^2} \right) = \alpha \nabla^2 u$

Just like in 1D, the Laplacian essentially measures how much the temperature at a specific point differs from the average temperature of all its neighbors in every direction.

Test Your Knowledge

1. Calculating the 2D Laplacian ▼

Question: Given the steady-state temperature distribution on a 2D plate $u(x,y) = x^2 - y^2$, compute the Laplacian $\nabla^2 u$. What physical situation does this represent?

Solution:

The 2D Laplacian is defined as $\nabla^2 u = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}$.

1. Differentiate with respect to $x$:
$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 2x$
$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = 2$

2. Differentiate with respect to $y$:
$\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -2y$
$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} = -2$

3. Add them together:
$\nabla^2 u = 2 + (-2) = 0$

Interpretation: Since the Laplacian is zero everywhere, $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = 0$. This means the temperature distribution $u(x,y) = x^2 - y^2$ is in perfect equilibrium and will not change over time. Equations where $\nabla^2 u = 0$ are known as Laplace's Equation.
