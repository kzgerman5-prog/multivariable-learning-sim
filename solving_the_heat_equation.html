The Three Constraints

Finding a mathematical solution to a Partial Differential Equation (PDE) isn't just about finding a function that makes the equation true. To describe a specific physical scenario, the function must satisfy exactly three constraints.

1. The PDE

The function must satisfy the physical law itself: $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$.

2. Initial Condition

The starting state of the system at time $t=0$. What does the temperature distribution look like right now? $u(x,0) = f(x)$.

3. Boundary Conditions

What happens at the edges of the rod ($x=0$ and $x=L$)? Are they fixed at ice-cold? Or are they insulated so no heat escapes?

1. Formulating a Boundary Value Problem ▼

Question: Formulate the complete boundary value problem for a rod of length $L=5$ with thermal diffusivity $\alpha = 2$. The rod initially has a uniform temperature of $100^\circ$C, but its ends are submerged in an ice bath ($0^\circ$C) for all $t > 0$.

Solution:

We need to state the PDE, the Boundary Conditions (BCs), and the Initial Condition (IC).

1. PDE: $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = 2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \quad \text{for } 0 < x < 5, \, t > 0$

2. BCs: The ends are fixed at $0^\circ$C. This is a Dirichlet condition.
$u(0, t) = 0 \quad \text{for } t > 0$
$u(5, t) = 0 \quad \text{for } t > 0$

3. IC: The initial temperature is $100$ everywhere.
$u(x, 0) = 100 \quad \text{for } 0 < x < 5$

Fourier's Three Steps

In 1822, Joseph Fourier figured out how to solve the Heat Equation by making three core, brilliant observations.

  • Certain sine waves offer simple solutions. If the temperature distribution looks exactly like a sine wave, the math becomes incredibly easy.
  • The principle of Superposition. The Heat Equation is linear. This means if you have two valid solutions, simply adding them together gives you a third valid solution.
  • Fourier Series. Almost any arbitrary function can be expressed as a sum of infinitely many sine and cosine waves.

The strategy? Break the complex initial temperature into a bunch of sine waves, figure out how each sine wave evolves over time independently, and add the results back together!

Test Your Knowledge

1. Proving Superposition (Linearity) ▼

Question: Prove that if $u_1(x,t)$ and $u_2(x,t)$ are solutions to the heat equation $u_t = \alpha u_{xx}$, then their sum $w(x,t) = u_1(x,t) + u_2(x,t)$ is also a solution.

Solution:

1. We are given that:
$\frac{\partial u_1}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 u_1}{\partial x^2}$
$\frac{\partial u_2}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 u_2}{\partial x^2}$

2. We want to test $w(x,t)$. Let's take its time derivative:
$\frac{\partial w}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}(u_1 + u_2) = \frac{\partial u_1}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial u_2}{\partial t}$

3. Substitute the given relations from step 1:
$\frac{\partial w}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 u_1}{\partial x^2} + \alpha \frac{\partial^2 u_2}{\partial x^2}$

4. Factor out $\alpha$ and use the linearity of the spatial derivative:
$\frac{\partial w}{\partial t} = \alpha \left( \frac{\partial^2 u_1}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u_2}{\partial x^2} \right) = \alpha \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}(u_1 + u_2) = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 w}{\partial x^2}$

Since $\frac{\partial w}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 w}{\partial x^2}$, $w(x,t)$ is a valid solution.

Why Sine Waves?

Why are sine waves the "cheat code" for the Heat Equation? It comes down to calculus. The Heat Equation relies on the second spatial derivative (the curvature).

If $u(x) = \sin(kx)$, then:

$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = -k^2 \sin(kx) = -k^2 u(x)$

The second derivative of a sine wave is exactly proportional to the negative of its height. Because the Heat Equation says the cooling rate is proportional to the second derivative, a sine wave distribution means that every single point cools at a rate exactly proportional to its current temperature. As a result, the entire wave scales down uniformly!

Test Your Knowledge

1. Analyzing Polynomials vs Sines ▼

Question: Consider an initial temperature distribution $u(x,0) = x^3 - x$. Will this shape simply scale down uniformly over time like a sine wave does? Show why or why not using the second derivative.

Solution:

For a shape to scale uniformly over time, its second derivative must be a constant multiple of itself, i.e., $u_{xx} = C \cdot u(x)$.

1. Let $u = x^3 - x$.

2. First derivative: $u_x = 3x^2 - 1$.

3. Second derivative: $u_{xx} = 6x$.

4. Does $6x = C(x^3 - x)$ for all $x$? No. There is no constant $C$ that makes this true.

Conclusion: Because $u_{xx}$ is not proportional to $u$, the cooling rate at different points is disjointed from their heights. The shape will warp and distort as it cools, rather than scaling down uniformly.

The Exponential Decay

We established that for a sine wave, $\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = -k^2 u$. If we substitute this into the Heat Equation, we get a fascinating result.

$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha (-k^2 u)$

This is now an Ordinary Differential Equation with respect to time! It says "the rate of change of $u$ is a negative constant times $u$." In calculus, whenever the rate of change is proportional to the amount present, the solution is an exponential function.

$u(t) = e^{-\alpha k^2 t}$

Test Your Knowledge

1. Solving the Separated ODE ▼

Question: Given the ODE $\frac{dT}{dt} = - \lambda T$ where $\lambda > 0$ is a constant, find the general solution for $T(t)$ using separation of variables.

Solution:

1. Separate the variables (get $T$ on one side, $t$ on the other):
$\frac{dT}{T} = -\lambda dt$

2. Integrate both sides:
$\int \frac{1}{T} dT = \int -\lambda dt$
$\ln|T| = -\lambda t + C_1$

3. Exponentiate both sides to solve for $T$:
$e^{\ln|T|} = e^{-\lambda t + C_1}$
$|T| = e^{C_1} \cdot e^{-\lambda t}$

4. Let $C = \pm e^{C_1}$ (a new arbitrary constant):
$T(t) = C e^{-\lambda t}$

The Boundary Detour

We have a beautiful solution: $u(x,t) = e^{-\alpha k^2 t} \sin(kx)$. But there is a major problem if we assume our rod is insulated at the ends (meaning no heat can flow in or out).

Heat flows down a temperature gradient. If heat cannot cross the boundary, the gradient (slope) exactly at the boundary must be zero. This is called a Neumann Boundary Condition:

$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(0, t) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(L, t) = 0$

A pure sine wave $\sin(kx)$ starts at $(0,0)$ with a steep slope. It violates the boundary condition immediately! If you put a sine wave of temperature in an insulated rod, heat would instantly try to flow out of the left tip.

Interactive: Why Sine Fails Neumann

Observe the sine wave at $x=0$. The tangent line is steep. Because the slope is not flat, heat (red arrow) is flowing across the boundary, violating the "insulated" assumption.

Fixing the Boundary Condition

To fix the left boundary, we simply shift to a cosine wave. Since the derivative of $\cos(kx)$ is $-k\sin(kx)$, evaluating it at $x=0$ yields exactly zero. It starts perfectly flat!

But what about the right boundary at $x=L$? We must choose our frequency $k$ very carefully so the wave naturally flattens out exactly when it reaches the right edge.

Test Your Knowledge

1. Verifying the Cosine Boundaries ▼

Question: Show mathematically that the function $u(x) = \cos(\frac{n\pi x}{L})$ for any integer $n$ satisfies the Neumann boundary conditions $u'(0) = 0$ and $u'(L) = 0$.

Solution:

1. First, find the spatial derivative $u'(x)$ using the chain rule:
$u'(x) = -\frac{n\pi}{L} \sin(\frac{n\pi x}{L})$

2. Check the left boundary at $x=0$:
$u'(0) = -\frac{n\pi}{L} \sin(0) = 0$. (Satisfied!)

3. Check the right boundary at $x=L$:
$u'(L) = -\frac{n\pi}{L} \sin(\frac{n\pi L}{L}) = -\frac{n\pi}{L} \sin(n\pi)$

4. Since $n$ is an integer, $n\pi$ is a multiple of $\pi$. The sine of any integer multiple of $\pi$ is exactly 0. Thus $u'(L) = 0$. (Satisfied!)

The Harmonics

Any integer value for $n$ produces a valid cosine wave that satisfies our insulated boundaries. These are the Harmonics of our system.

Because the decay rate in time depends on $k^2$ (and $k = \frac{n\pi}{L}$), higher frequency harmonics have much sharper curvature and therefore decay exponentially faster than lower frequency ones.

$u_n(x,t) = \cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \cdot e^{-\alpha \left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2 t}$

Test Your Knowledge

1. Comparing Decay Rates ▼

Question: For a rod of length $L=\pi$ and $\alpha=1$, how much faster does the amplitude of the 3rd harmonic ($n=3$) decay compared to the 1st harmonic ($n=1$)?

Solution:

The general decay term is $e^{-\alpha (\frac{n\pi}{L})^2 t}$. Given $L=\pi$ and $\alpha=1$, this simplifies to $e^{-n^2 t}$.

1. For $n=1$, the amplitude is $A_1(t) = e^{-1^2 t} = e^{-t}$.

2. For $n=3$, the amplitude is $A_3(t) = e^{-3^2 t} = e^{-9t}$.

Conclusion: The 3rd harmonic decays with a rate constant of 9, which is nine times faster than the 1st harmonic. This perfectly illustrates why sharp jagged edges in initial temperature distributions smooth out almost instantly.
