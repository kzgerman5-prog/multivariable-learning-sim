CHAPTER 03 · POLES, FORCES, AND RESPONSE

Solving differential equations

The Laplace transform turns derivatives into algebra and carries initial conditions along for the ride.

1. Derivatives become multiplication by s

For a function f(t), its one-sided Laplace transform is F(s)=∫₀∞f(t)e−stdt. To transform a derivative, integrate by parts: choose u=e−st and dv=f′(t)dt. The boundary term remembers the value at time zero:

ℒ{f′}=∫₀∞f′e−stdt = [f e−st]0∞+s∫₀∞f e−stdt = sF(s)−f(0).

Assuming the far boundary vanishes, the derivative has become multiplication by s minus the initial position. Applying the same rule once more gives ℒ{f″}=s²F(s)−s f(0)−f′(0). Higher derivatives bring in all the initial data. This is the key: we do not discard the starting state; it appears automatically in the transformed equation.

THE INITIAL-VALUE RULESℒ{f′}=sF−f(0)   ·   ℒ{f″}=s²F−s f(0)−f′(0)Differential operators turn into polynomials in s, while initial conditions become ordinary terms.

2. Transform a spring driven by a force

Consider a unit-mass spring with damping: x″+2ζω₀x′+ω₀²x = F₀ cos(Ωt). Here ω₀ is the spring’s natural angular frequency, ζ is the dimensionless damping ratio, Ω is the forcing frequency, and F₀ is the force amplitude. Give it initial displacement x₀ and velocity v₀.

Write X(s)=ℒ{x}. Transform each term, keeping the initial data visible:

(s²X−s x₀−v₀)+2ζω₀(sX−x₀)+ω₀²X = F₀s/(s²+Ω²).

Collect X(s). Define D(s)=s²+2ζω₀s+ω₀², the characteristic polynomial of the unforced spring. Then

THE TRANSFORMED SOLUTIONX(s)=[F₀s/(s²+Ω²)+(s+2ζω₀)x₀+v₀]/D(s)For zero initial conditions, X(s)=F₀s/[(s²+Ω²)D(s)].

The denominator shows two sets of poles: the natural roots of D(s), which describe the spring’s own motion, and the forcing poles ±iΩ. With positive damping, natural roots have negative real parts, so their transient motion fades away. The forcing leaves a persistent oscillation at frequency Ω.

3. Invert the algebra and separate transient from steady state

To recover x(t), decompose X(s) into simpler fractions whose inverse transforms are known. For distinct roots, a term A/(s−p) becomes Aept. Conjugate poles combine into real damped sinusoids; imaginary forcing poles combine into sines and cosines. Repeated poles produce powers of t multiplying exponentials.

A useful example has zero initial conditions and forcing F₀cos(Ωt):

X(s)=F₀s / [(s²+Ω²)(s²+2ζω₀s+ω₀²)].

When the forcing frequency is away from resonance, partial fractions give a natural, damped transient plus a sinusoidal steady state. At long times the transient becomes tiny and only the forced response remains. Near resonance (Ω close to ω₀ for light damping), the steady-state amplitude becomes large. Damping prevents the ideal unbounded growth predicted by the undamped exactly resonant model.

The same pole picture also diagnoses stability: poles left of the imaginary axis decay, poles on it persist, and right-half-plane poles grow. For a causal, finite-dimensional system, its natural poles explain what happens after a disturbance, while input poles explain what rhythms the forcing supplies.

4. Why transform methods are systematic

Guessing est works for constant-coefficient linear equations because differentiation preserves an exponential’s shape. The Laplace transform packages that fact into a method that also handles forcing and initial conditions. It is especially effective for piecewise inputs such as a switch turning on at a known time, impulses, and sudden changes.

The workflow is: transform each term, substitute initial conditions, solve the algebraic equation for X(s), factor the denominator, use partial fractions, and invert each term. The transform does not make every problem effortless: finding roots or decomposing a high-degree rational function can still be difficult. But it turns the central operation—solving a linear initial-value problem—into a repeatable set of algebraic steps.

CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Three problems, fully worked

01 Solve y′+2y=0, y(0)=3, using Laplace transforms.

Step 1 — transform the equation. Let Y(s)=ℒ{y}. Then sY−y(0)+2Y=0. Substituting y(0)=3 gives (s+2)Y=3.

Step 2 — isolate and invert. Y=3/(s+2), which matches 3/(s−(−2)). Therefore

y(t)=3e−2t.

The pole at −2 encodes the decay rate; the initial condition sets its coefficient.

02 Solve x″+x=1 with x(0)=0, x′(0)=0. Check the initial data.

Step 1 — transform. s²X−0−0+X=1/s, so X=1/[s(s²+1)].

Step 2 — partial fractions. X=1/s−s/(s²+1). Inverting gives

x(t)=1−cos t.

Step 3 — verify. x(0)=0; x′(t)=sin t so x′(0)=0. Also x″+x=cos t+(1−cos t)=1. Each condition is satisfied.

03 A unit mass obeys x″+2x′+5x=cos(2t), x(0)=x′(0)=0. Find X(s) and describe its poles.

Step 1 — transform both sides. The initial terms vanish, and ℒ{cos 2t}=s/(s²+4). Thus

(s²+2s+5)X(s)=s/(s²+4),   X(s)=s/[(s²+4)(s²+2s+5)].

Step 2 — locate the poles. The forcing gives ±2i. The natural polynomial s²+2s+5 has roots −1±2i. So the response has an oscillatory forcing component at frequency 2 and a transient that oscillates at frequency 2 while decaying as e−t. Since the damping ratio is positive, the transient disappears and only the frequency-2 steady response remains.

This identifies the structure without carrying out all the partial fractions. To obtain exact sine and cosine coefficients, one would decompose X(s) at these four distinct poles.
